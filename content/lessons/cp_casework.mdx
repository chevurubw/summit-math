---
title: Casework and complements
---

Two strategies handle most counting problems that don't fit a formula. **Casework** splits the problem into smaller pieces you can count separately. **Complementary counting** counts what you **don't** want and subtracts. Knowing which to use is half the battle.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- split a problem into cases that don't overlap and don't miss anything,
- choose a good variable to split on,
- recognize "at least one" as a signal for the complement,
- check your casework for gaps and double counts.

## Casework

Good cases are **disjoint** (no outcome is in two cases) and **complete** (every outcome is in some case). Then you just add.

<Steps problem="In how many ways can you make 25 cents using pennies, nickels and dimes?">
<Step title="Split on the biggest coin">
Dimes can be 0, 1 or 2. Once dimes and nickels are chosen, pennies fill the rest in exactly one way.
</Step>
<Step title="0 dimes">
Nickels: 0 to 5. That's 6 ways.
</Step>
<Step title="1 dime">
15 cents left; nickels 0 to 3. That's 4 ways.
</Step>
<Step title="2 dimes">
5 cents left; nickels 0 or 1. That's 2 ways.
</Step>
<Step title="Add">
$6 + 4 + 2 = 12$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Tip title="Split on the most restrictive thing">
Case on the variable with the fewest possible values, or the one that makes the rest simplest. In the coin problem, splitting on dimes gives only 3 cases.
</Tip>

How many two digit numbers have digits that add up to 5?
</Check>

## Complementary counting

$$
\text{what you want} = \text{everything} - \text{what you don't want}
$$

This is best when "what you want" has **many** cases and its opposite has **few**. The signal phrase is "**at least one**": its opposite, "none", is a single simple case.

<Steps problem="How many three digit numbers contain at least one 7?">
<Step title="Count everything">
There are 900 three digit numbers.
</Step>
<Step title="Count the opposite: no 7 at all">
First digit: 1 to 9 except 7, so 8 choices. Other digits: 0 to 9 except 7, so 9 each. That's $8 \cdot 9 \cdot 9 = 648$.
</Step>
<Step title="Subtract">
$900 - 648 = 252$.
</Step>
</Steps>

Directly, you'd need cases for one 7, two 7s and three 7s in different positions. The complement takes one line.

<Check choices={["$5$", "$10$", "$11$", "$12$", "$15$"]} answer={2} explain="16 outcomes in total. The opposite of 'at least two heads' is 'zero or one head': $1 + 4 = 5$ outcomes. So $16 - 5 = 11$.">
A coin is flipped 4 times. In how many of the 16 outcomes are there at least two heads?
</Check>

## Casework and complements together

Big problems often mix both: complement first to simplify, then casework on what remains.

<Trap>
After casework, sanity check: are any outcomes in two cases (double counting)? Did any outcome fall through the cracks? Testing your cases on a small version of the problem catches both.
</Trap>

<Check choices={["$144$", "$180$", "$200$", "$216$", "$252$"]} answer={2} explain="All three digit numbers with different digits: $9 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 648$. Those with no 5: the first digit has 8 choices (1 to 9 except 5), the second has 8 (0 to 9 except 5 and the first digit), the third has 7. That is $448$. So $648 - 448 = 200$.">
How many three digit numbers with all different digits contain the digit 5?
</Check>

<Recap>
- Casework: split into disjoint, complete cases and add. Split on the most restrictive choice.
- Complement: everything minus the opposite. Use it when the opposite is simpler, especially for "at least one".
- Check for overlaps and gaps after casework.
</Recap>
