title: Counting principles
---

Counting problems ask "how many?" The answer is often too big to list, so you need principles that count **without listing**. Just two rules, multiplication and addition, power everything else in this unit.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- use the multiplication principle for choices made one after another,
- use the addition principle for separate cases,
- count the numbers in a list without error,
- handle restrictions by filling the pickiest slot first.

## The multiplication principle

If one choice can be made in $a$ ways, and then a second choice can be made in $b$ ways **no matter what the first choice was**, the two choices together can be made in $a \times b$ ways.

A tree diagram shows why. Two shirts and three pairs of pants: each shirt branches into 3 outfits, so $2 \times 3 = 6$ outfits.

## Restrictions: fill the pickiest slot first

When some positions have rules, make the most restricted choice first. Then the later choices are easy to count.

<Steps problem="How many three digit numbers have all odd digits?">
<Step>
Each of the three digits must be one of $1, 3, 5, 7, 9$: 5 choices each. (No zero issue, since 0 is even.)
</Step>
<Step>
$5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 = 125$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Steps problem="How many three digit even numbers have all different digits?">
<Step title="Why order matters here">
The last digit must be even, the first digit can't be 0, and the digits can't repeat. The 0 creates a conflict between the first and last slots, so split into cases.
</Step>
<Step title="Case 1: last digit is 0">
Last: 1 way. First: any of the 9 other digits. Middle: 8 left. Total $9 \cdot 8 = 72$.
</Step>
<Step title="Case 2: last digit is 2, 4, 6 or 8">
Last: 4 ways. First: not 0 and not the last digit, so 8 ways. Middle: 8 ways (0 is now allowed). Total $4 \cdot 8 \cdot 8 = 256$.
</Step>
<Step title="Add the cases">
$72 + 256 = 328$.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The addition principle

If you can do something in one of several **separate** ways (cases that can't happen together), add the counts. The example above used it: case 1 **or** case 2.

<KeyIdea>
"And" (choices made in sequence) means multiply. "Or" (separate cases) means add. Most counting problems are a mix.
</KeyIdea>

## Counting lists

How many numbers are in $14, 17, 20, \ldots, 98$? Shift and scale it into $1, 2, 3, \ldots$:

- subtract 11: $3, 6, 9, \ldots, 87$,
- divide by 3: $1, 2, 3, \ldots, 29$.

So there are 29 numbers. The shortcut: $\dfrac{\text{last} - \text{first}}{\text{step}} + 1 = \dfrac{98 - 14}{3} + 1 = 29$.

<Trap title="Fence posts">
The numbers from 5 to 12 are $12 - 5 + 1 = 8$ numbers, not 7. A fence 10 meters long with posts every meter has 11 posts. Always ask: do I include both ends?
</Trap>

<Check choices={["$27$", "$28$", "$29$", "$30$", "$85$"]} answer={2} explain="$\tfrac{98 - 14}{3} + 1 = 28 + 1 = 29$.">
How many numbers are in the list $14, 17, 20, \ldots, 98$?
</Check>

<Check choices={["$648$", "$720$", "$729$", "$810$", "$900$"]} answer={0} explain="First digit: 1 to 9 (9 ways). Second: any digit except the first (9 ways, since 0 is now allowed). Third: 8 ways. $9 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 648$.">

<Tip>
Before multiplying, ask: "Does the number of choices for this slot depend on earlier choices?" If yes, either reorder the slots or split into cases.
</Tip>

<Recap>
- Sequential choices: multiply. Separate cases: add.
- Fill the most restricted slot first; split into cases when slots conflict.
- Count lists with $\frac{\text{last} - \text{first}}{\text{step}} + 1$.
- Watch for fence post errors: include both ends?
</Recap>
