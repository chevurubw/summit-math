title: Divisibility rules
---

A **divisibility rule** tells you whether a number divides another just by looking at its digits. On the AMC they power the classic "find the missing digit" problems. You will also learn **why** each rule works, so you can invent a rule when you need one.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- test divisibility by 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10 and 11 in seconds,
- combine rules to test 6, 12, 15, 36, 72 and more,
- solve missing digit problems,
- explain why the rules work.

## The rules

| Divisible by | Test |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | last digit is even |
| 3 | sum of digits divisible by 3 |
| 4 | last **two** digits form a multiple of 4 |
| 5 | last digit is 0 or 5 |
| 8 | last **three** digits form a multiple of 8 |
| 9 | sum of digits divisible by 9 |
| 10 | last digit is 0 |
| 11 | alternating sum of digits ($+ - + - \cdots$ from the right) divisible by 11 |

## Why they work

**2, 4, 8 and 5, 10:** $10$ is divisible by 2, $100$ by 4, and $1000$ by 8. So everything except the last one, two or three digits is automatically a multiple. Only the end matters.

**3 and 9:** each power of 10 is one more than a multiple of 9 ($10 = 9 + 1$, $100 = 99 + 1$, ...). So

$$
4725 = 4 \cdot 1000 + 7 \cdot 100 + 2 \cdot 10 + 5 = (\text{multiple of } 9) + (4 + 7 + 2 + 5).
$$

A number and its digit sum leave the **same remainder** when divided by 9 (or 3).

**11:** powers of 10 alternate between one more and one less than multiples of 11 ($10 = 11 - 1$, $100 = 99 + 1$, ...). That's where the alternating signs come from.

<KeyIdea>
The digit sum rule tells you more than yes or no: it gives the **remainder**. $4725$ has digit sum $18$, so it is divisible by 9. $4726$ would leave remainder 1.
</KeyIdea>

## Combining rules

To test divisibility by a composite number, split it into factors that **share no common factor**, and test each.

- 6: test 2 and 3.
- 12: test 3 and 4 (not 2 and 6, since they overlap).
- 72: test 8 and 9.

<Trap>
Divisible by 4 and by 6 does **not** mean divisible by 24. Take 12: divisible by 4 and 6, but not 24. The factors 4 and 6 share a 2. You only get $\text{lcm}(4, 6) = 12$.
</Trap>

<Steps problem="How many four digit numbers of the form $6A4B$ are divisible by 72?">
<Step title="Split 72">
$72 = 8 \cdot 9$, and 8 and 9 share no factor. So test 8 and 9.
</Step>
<Step title="Test 9">
Digit sum $6 + A + 4 + B = 10 + A + B$ must be 18 or 27, so $A + B = 8$ or $A + B = 17$.
</Step>
<Step title="Test 8">
The last three digits $A4B$ must be a multiple of 8. So $B$ is even. Check each pair: $A + B = 8$ gives $840$ ✓, $642$, $444$, $246$, $048$ ✓. And $A + B = 17$ with even $B$ gives only $948$, which fails.
</Step>
<Step title="Count">
$6840$ and $6048$. That's 2 numbers. (Check: $6840 = 72 \cdot 95$ and $6048 = 72 \cdot 84$.)
</Step>
</Steps>

A number is divisible by both 4 and 6. Which of these must also divide it?
</Check>

## Remainders by 9 as a checking tool

Because a number and its digit sum match modulo 9, you can check arithmetic: $38 \times 47 = 1786$? Digit sums: $38 \to 2$, $47 \to 2$, product $\to 4$; and $1786 \to 22 \to 4$ ✓. This is called **casting out nines**, and it catches most multiplication slips.

<Tip>
Missing digit problems almost always use 9 and 11 together, or 8 and 9 together. Write the digit sum condition and the alternating sum condition as small equations, then try the few digits that fit.
</Tip>

<Recap>
- 2, 4, 8: look at the last 1, 2, 3 digits. 5 and 10: last digit.
- 3 and 9: digit sum, which also gives the remainder.
- 11: alternating digit sum.
- For composites, test factors that share no common factor.
- Missing digit problems become small equations in the unknown digits.
</Recap>
