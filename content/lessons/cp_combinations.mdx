title: Combinations

A **combination** is a selection where **order does not matter**: a committee, a hand of cards, a pizza's toppings. Choosing Ana, Ben and Cal is the same committee as choosing Cal, Ana and Ben. Combinations are the most used tool in all of AMC counting.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- compute $\binom{n}{k}$ and explain where the formula comes from,
- count committees with conditions,
- count paths on a grid,
- use "stars and bars" to split identical items among people.

## From permutations to combinations

Choose 3 people from 8 **in order**: $8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 = 336$. But each committee of 3 was counted once for every way to order its members, which is $3! = 6$ times. So the number of committees is $\tfrac{336}{6} = 56$.

<Formula>

$$
\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n - k)!} = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!}
$$

Read it "$n$ choose $k$". Quick way: multiply $k$ numbers counting down from $n$, then divide by $k!$.
</Formula>

Two facts that save time:

- **Symmetry:** $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$. Choosing 3 to be on the committee is the same as choosing 5 to leave off: $\binom{8}{3} = \binom{8}{5}$.
- **Pascal's rule:** $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}$. Either a particular person is chosen or not.

</Check>

## Committees with conditions

Split the choice into independent parts and multiply.

<Steps problem="A committee of 4 is chosen from 6 boys and 5 girls. How many committees have exactly 2 girls?">
<Step>
Choose the girls: $\binom{5}{2} = 10$.
</Step>
<Step>
Choose the boys: $\binom{6}{2} = 15$.
</Step>
<Step>
Multiply: $10 \cdot 15 = 150$.
</Step>
</Steps>

For "**at least** one girl", use the complement: all committees minus all boy committees, $\binom{11}{4} - \binom{6}{4} = 330 - 15 = 315$.

<Trap>
Don't count "at least one girl" as "pick one girl, then any 3 others" ($5 \cdot \binom{10}{3}$). That counts a committee with two girls twice, once for each girl you could have picked first.
</Trap>

## Paths on a grid

How many shortest paths go from the bottom left to the top right of a grid that is 4 wide and 3 tall? Every path is a string of 4 Rights and 3 Ups, in some order, like RRURUUR. Choose which 3 of the 7 steps are Ups: $\binom{7}{3} = 35$.

The grid below fills in the count at each point. Each point can only be reached from the left or from below, so its count is the **sum of those two**. That's Pascal's triangle, tilted.

## Stars and bars

How many ways can 10 identical candies be given to 3 kids? Line up the 10 candies (stars) and insert 2 dividers (bars) to split them into 3 groups:

$$
\star\star\star \mid \star\star\star\star\star \mid \star\star \quad \text{means } 3, 5, 2.
$$

Every arrangement of 10 stars and 2 bars is one way to split. Choose positions for the 2 bars among 12 spots: $\binom{12}{2} = 66$.

<Formula title="Stars and bars">
The number of ways to split $n$ identical items among $k$ people (some may get zero) is

$$
\binom{n + k - 1}{k - 1}.
$$

If everyone must get **at least one**, hand each person one first, then split the remaining $n - k$.
</Formula>

<Check choices={["$36$", "$45$", "$55$", "$66$", "$78$"]} answer={0} explain="Give each kid 1 first, leaving 7 to split freely among 3: $\binom{7 + 2}{2} = \binom{9}{2} = 36$.">
How many ways can 10 identical candies be given to 3 kids so that each kid gets at least one?
</Check>

<KeyIdea title="Order or not?">
Ask: if I swap two of my choices, do I get a different outcome? If yes, it's a permutation. If no, it's a combination. Committees, hands and groups are combinations; lineups, rankings and passwords are permutations.
</KeyIdea>

<Recap>
- $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$: choices where order doesn't matter.
- Symmetry $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$; Pascal's rule adds neighbors.
- Conditions: choose each group separately and multiply. "At least one": complement.
- Grid paths: $\binom{\text{total steps}}{\text{up steps}}$, or add counts point by point.
- Stars and bars: $\binom{n + k - 1}{k - 1}$.
</Recap>
