title: 3D solids
---

A **solid** is a three dimensional shape. For each kind you need two numbers: its **volume** (how much it holds, in cubic units) and its **surface area** (how much paper would wrap it, in square units). The most important skill is not memorizing formulas but **finding a right triangle hidden inside the solid**.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- find volumes and surface areas of prisms, cylinders, pyramids, cones and spheres,
- find the long diagonal of a box,
- find a cone's slant height,
- predict how volume changes when you scale a shape.

## The formulas

| Solid | Volume | Surface area |
| --- | --- | --- |
| box $a \times b \times c$ | $abc$ | $2(ab + bc + ca)$ |
| cube, edge $s$ | $s^3$ | $6s^2$ |
| prism or cylinder | base area $\times$ height | two bases plus the sides |
| cylinder, radius $r$, height $h$ | $\pi r^2 h$ | $2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h$ |
| pyramid or cone | $\tfrac{1}{3}$ base area $\times$ height | base plus the slanted faces |
| cone, radius $r$, slant $\ell$ | $\tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ | $\pi r^2 + \pi r \ell$ |
| sphere, radius $r$ | $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ | $4\pi r^2$ |

<KeyIdea title="Two ideas cover almost everything">
1. **Prism rule:** a shape with the same cross section all the way up has volume $=$ base area $\times$ height.
2. **Pointy rule:** a shape that tapers to a point (pyramid, cone) has exactly $\tfrac{1}{3}$ of that.
</KeyIdea>

## Cones: the slant height

A cone's height $h$, its radius $r$ and its **slant height** $\ell$ (from the tip down the side to the rim) form a right triangle:

$$
r^2 + h^2 = \ell^2.
$$

Use $h$ for the volume and $\ell$ for the curved surface area. Mixing them up is the classic error.

<Steps problem="A cone has radius 6 and slant height 10. Find its volume.">
<Step title="Find the height">
$h = \sqrt{10^2 - 6^2} = 8$.
</Step>
<Step title="Volume">
$\tfrac{1}{3}\pi \cdot 6^2 \cdot 8 = 96\pi$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Trap>
Volume formulas use the **perpendicular height**, never the slant height. Surface area formulas for cones and pyramids use the **slant height**.
</Trap>

## Scaling

If every length of a solid is multiplied by $k$, its surface area multiplies by $k^2$ and its volume by $k^3$. Double the radius of a sphere and it holds $2^3 = 8$ times as much.

<Check choices={["$2$", "$4$", "$6$", "$8$", "$16$"]} answer={1} explain="Volume ratio $64 = k^3$ gives $k = 4$, and the radius scales by the length factor $k = 4$.">
One sphere has 64 times the volume of another. How many times larger is its radius?
</Check>

## Melting and pouring

When a solid is melted and recast, or water is poured from one container to another, **volume stays the same**. Set the two volume formulas equal.

<Check choices={["$1$", "$2$", "$3$", "$4$", "$6$"]} answer={3} explain="The metal's volume stays the same: $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi \cdot 3^3 = 36\pi$. In the cylinder: $\pi \cdot 3^2 \cdot h = 36\pi$, so $h = 4$.">
A solid metal ball of radius 3 is melted down and recast as a solid cylinder of radius 3. How tall is the cylinder?
</Check>

<Tip>
For surface area of a weird solid, count faces systematically: front and back, left and right, top and bottom. For volume of a weird solid, think "big box minus what's missing".
</Tip>

<Recap>
- Prism or cylinder: base area times height. Pyramid or cone: one third of that.
- Sphere: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, $S = 4\pi r^2$.
- Space diagonal $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}$; cone $r^2 + h^2 = \ell^2$.
- Scale by $k$: area $\times k^2$, volume $\times k^3$.
- Melting and pouring keep the volume.
</Recap>
