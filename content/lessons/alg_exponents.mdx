title: Exponents and radicals
---

An exponent is shorthand for repeated multiplication: $2^5 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 32$. Almost every exponent rule comes from simply **counting how many copies** are being multiplied. If you understand that, you never have to memorize the rules.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- use the exponent rules without memorizing them,
- explain what zero, negative and fractional exponents mean,
- simplify square roots and clear roots from denominators,
- compare huge powers like $2^{60}$ and $3^{40}$ without a calculator.

## The rules are just counting

| Rule | Why it's true |
| --- | --- |
| $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$ | $m$ copies times $n$ more copies is $m + n$ copies |
| $\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$ | $n$ copies cancel from the top |
| $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$ | $n$ groups of $m$ copies each |
| $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$ | rearrange the $n$ copies of $ab$ |

<Trap>
$(a + b)^2$ is **not** $a^2 + b^2$. Exponents spread over multiplication, never over addition: $(3 + 4)^2 = 49$ but $3^2 + 4^2 = 25$.
</Trap>

## Zero, negative and fractional exponents

Look at the powers of 2 going down: $8, 4, 2, \ldots$ Each step left divides by 2. Keep going and the pattern forces the meaning of the strange exponents:

$$
2^3 = 8,\quad 2^2 = 4,\quad 2^1 = 2,\quad 2^0 = 1,\quad 2^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2},\quad 2^{-2} = \tfrac{1}{4}.
$$

So $a^0 = 1$ and $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$. Slide the base and watch every curve pass through $(0, 1)$.

**Fractional exponents are roots.** Since $(a^{1/2})^2 = a^1 = a$, the number $a^{1/2}$ must be $\sqrt{a}$. In general

$$
a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}, \qquad a^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m.
$$

<Tip title="Take the root first">
For $8^{2/3}$, take the cube root first ($\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$), then square ($2^2 = 4$). Small numbers are easier than squaring 8 first and then finding $\sqrt[3]{64}$.
</Tip>

## Same base is the master key

Most exponent equations on the AMC become easy once **both sides use the same base**. Rewrite 4, 8, 16, 32 as powers of 2; rewrite 9, 27, 81 as powers of 3.

<Steps problem="Solve $4^{x+1} = 8^{x}$.">
<Step title="Write both sides with base 2">
$4 = 2^2$ and $8 = 2^3$, so the equation is $2^{2(x+1)} = 2^{3x}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Equal powers of the same base: match the exponents">
$2x + 2 = 3x$, so $x = 2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
$4^3 = 64$ and $8^2 = 64$ ✓.
</Step>
</Steps>

### Comparing giant powers

Which is biggest: $2^{60}$, $3^{40}$, or $5^{20}$? Make the **exponents** match. All three exponents are multiples of 20:

$$
2^{60} = (2^3)^{20} = 8^{20}, \qquad 3^{40} = (3^2)^{20} = 9^{20}, \qquad 5^{20}.
$$

Now compare the bases: $9 > 8 > 5$, so $3^{40}$ is the largest.

The biggest base is 27, so $3^{75}$ wins.">

</Check>

## Simplifying square roots

Pull out the **largest perfect square factor**:

$$
\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{36}\,\sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2}.
$$

Like roots combine like like terms: $3\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{2} = 8\sqrt{2}$, but $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ cannot be combined.

### Clearing a root from the bottom

To remove a root from a denominator, multiply top and bottom by something that makes the root vanish:

- one root: $\dfrac{6}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{6\sqrt{3}}{3} = 2\sqrt{3}$,
- a sum or difference: use the **conjugate**, because $(a - b)(a + b) = a^2 - b^2$.

<Steps problem="Simplify $\dfrac{4}{\sqrt{5} - 1}$.">
<Step>
Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate $\sqrt{5} + 1$.
</Step>
<Step>
Bottom: $(\sqrt{5} - 1)(\sqrt{5} + 1) = 5 - 1 = 4$. Top: $4(\sqrt{5} + 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Cancel the 4: the answer is $\sqrt{5} + 1$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Check choices={["$3\\sqrt{2}$", "$5\\sqrt{2}$", "$7\\sqrt{2}$", "$9\\sqrt{2}$", "$\\sqrt{68}$"]} answer={2} explain="$\sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$, so the sum is $7\sqrt{2}$. You cannot add under the root: $\sqrt{50 + 8} = \sqrt{58}$ is a different number.">

<Recap>
- Exponent rules come from counting copies: add when multiplying, subtract when dividing, multiply for a power of a power.
- $a^0 = 1$, $a^{-n} = \tfrac{1}{a^n}$, $a^{m/n} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^m$.
- Exponents never spread over addition.
- To solve exponent equations, rewrite with the same base and match exponents.
- To compare powers, rewrite with the same exponent and compare bases.
- Simplify roots by pulling out the largest square factor; rationalize with the conjugate.
</Recap>
