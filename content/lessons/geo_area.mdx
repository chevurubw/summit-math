title: Area
---

**Area** measures how much flat space a shape covers, in square units. You need only a few formulas, but the AMC rewards knowing **why** they work, because then you can split, slide and subtract shapes to find areas no formula covers directly.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- use the area formulas for triangles and the standard quadrilaterals,
- choose any side of a triangle as the base,
- compare areas of triangles that share a height,
- find the area of strange shapes by adding and subtracting pieces.

## The formulas

| Shape | Area | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rectangle | $bh$ | rows times columns of unit squares |
| parallelogram | $bh$ | slice off a triangle and slide it over to make a rectangle |
| triangle | $\tfrac{1}{2}bh$ | half of a parallelogram |
| trapezoid | $\tfrac{1}{2}(b_1 + b_2)h$ | average base times height |
| rhombus, kite | $\tfrac{1}{2}d_1 d_2$ | half the rectangle around the diagonals |
| circle | $\pi r^2$ | see the circles lesson |

The **height** is always measured **perpendicular** to the base, not along a slanted side.

## Sliding the top doesn't change the area

Keep the base fixed and slide the top vertex along a line parallel to the base. The height never changes, so the area never changes, even though the triangle looks completely different.

<KeyIdea title="Triangles sharing a height">
Triangles with the same height have areas in the same ratio as their bases. If point $D$ splits side $BC$ into $BD = 2$ and $DC = 5$, then $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle ADC$ have areas in the ratio $2 : 5$, because they share the height from $A$.
</KeyIdea>

## Any side can be the base

A triangle has three bases and three heights, and $\tfrac{1}{2}bh$ gives the same area for each pair. That gives a powerful trick: **compute the area one way, then use it to find a height you couldn't measure**.

<Steps problem="A triangle has sides 13, 14 and 15. Its area is 84. How long is the altitude to the side of length 14?">
<Step>
Use the side 14 as the base: $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 14 \cdot h = 84$.
</Step>
<Step>
$h = \dfrac{168}{14} = 12$.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Composite shapes: add or subtract

For odd shapes, either **cut them into pieces** you know, or **draw a box around them and subtract** the extra corners.

<Steps problem="Find the area of the triangle with vertices $(1, 1)$, $(7, 3)$ and $(3, 6)$.">
<Step title="Box it in">
The smallest rectangle around it goes from $x = 1$ to $7$ and $y = 1$ to $6$: area $6 \times 5 = 30$.
</Step>
<Step title="Subtract the three right triangles in the corners">
Bottom right: legs $6$ and $2$, area $6$. Top right: legs $4$ and $3$, area $6$. Left: legs $2$ and $5$, area $5$.
</Step>
<Step title="Subtract">
$30 - 6 - 6 - 5 = 13$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Formula title="The Shoelace Formula">
For a polygon with vertices $(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)$ listed in order around the shape,

$$
\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\left| x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1 + x_2 y_3 - x_3 y_2 + \cdots + x_n y_1 - x_1 y_n \right|.
$$

For the triangle above: $\tfrac{1}{2}|1 \cdot 3 - 7 \cdot 1 + 7 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 6| = \tfrac{1}{2}|26| = 13$ ✓.
</Formula>

## Two special areas to remember

- **Equilateral triangle** with side $s$: $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}s^2$.
- **Regular hexagon** with side $s$: six equilateral triangles, $\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}s^2$.

<Recap>
- Triangle $\tfrac{1}{2}bh$, parallelogram $bh$, trapezoid $\tfrac{1}{2}(b_1 + b_2)h$, kite or rhombus $\tfrac{1}{2}d_1 d_2$.
- Height is perpendicular to the base. Sliding a vertex parallel to the base keeps the area.
- Triangles with the same height have areas proportional to their bases.
- Use one area to find an unknown height.
- For odd shapes: split, or box and subtract. On a grid, Shoelace works for any polygon.
</Recap>
