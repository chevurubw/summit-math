---
title: Primes and divisors
---

A **prime** is a whole number greater than 1 whose only divisors are 1 and itself: $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, \ldots$ Every other whole number greater than 1 breaks into primes in exactly one way. That single fact, the **prime factorization**, is the master key to number theory on the AMC.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- factor any number into primes quickly,
- test whether a number is prime,
- count the divisors of a number without listing them,
- add up all the divisors of a number.

## Prime factorization

Split a number into any two factors, then keep splitting until only primes remain. The result is the same no matter how you start.

$$
360 = 36 \cdot 10 = (4 \cdot 9)(2 \cdot 5) = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5.
$$

**Is it prime?** To test $n$, try dividing by primes up to $\sqrt{n}$. If none work, $n$ is prime. For $n = 221$: $\sqrt{221} < 15$, so try $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13$. And $221 = 13 \cdot 17$, so it is not prime.

<Trap>
1 is **not** prime, and 2 is the **only** even prime. Many problems hinge on the fact that every other prime is odd.
</Trap>

<Tip>
Know the primes under 100 cold (there are 25 of them), and a few tricky composites: $91 = 7 \cdot 13$, $119 = 7 \cdot 17$, $133 = 7 \cdot 19$, $143 = 11 \cdot 13$, $221 = 13 \cdot 17$, $1001 = 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13$.
</Tip>

## Counting divisors without listing them

Every divisor of $72 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2$ looks like $2^a \cdot 3^b$, where $a$ is any of $0, 1, 2, 3$ (four choices) and $b$ is any of $0, 1, 2$ (three choices). Here they all are, laid out in a grid:

| | $3^0$ | $3^1$ | $3^2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $2^0$ | 1 | 3 | 9 |
| $2^1$ | 2 | 6 | 18 |
| $2^2$ | 4 | 12 | 36 |
| $2^3$ | 8 | 24 | 72 |

Four rows times three columns: $4 \cdot 3 = 12$ divisors.

<Formula title="Number of divisors">
If $n = p^a \cdot q^b \cdot r^c \cdots$, then $n$ has

$$
(a + 1)(b + 1)(c + 1)\cdots
$$

divisors. Add one to each exponent and multiply.
</Formula>

<Check choices={["$12$", "$18$", "$20$", "$24$", "$36$"]} answer={3} explain="$360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^1$, so the count is $(3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 24$.">
How many positive divisors does 360 have?
</Check>

<Steps problem="What is the smallest positive integer with exactly 6 divisors?">
<Step title="How can 6 be a product of (exponent + 1) factors?">
$6 = 6$, giving $n = p^5$; or $6 = 3 \cdot 2$, giving $n = p^2 q$.
</Step>
<Step title="Make each form as small as possible">
$p^5$: smallest is $2^5 = 32$. $p^2 q$: put the bigger exponent on the smallest prime, $2^2 \cdot 3 = 12$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
$12 < 32$, so the answer is $12$. Its divisors: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$ ✓.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Perfect squares have an odd number of divisors

Divisors come in pairs $d$ and $\tfrac{n}{d}$: for 12, the pairs are $(1, 12)$, $(2, 6)$, $(3, 4)$. The only way a divisor has no partner is if $d = \tfrac{n}{d}$, meaning $n = d^2$. So **only perfect squares have an odd number of divisors**. (In the formula, every exponent of a square is even, so every $a + 1$ is odd.)

<Check choices={["$9$", "$10$", "$11$", "$25$", "$50$"]} answer={1} explain="Only perfect squares have an odd number of divisors, and the squares from 1 to 100 are $1^2, 2^2, \ldots, 10^2$: ten of them.">
How many integers from 1 to 100 have an odd number of positive divisors?
</Check>

This pairing also gives the **product** of all divisors: the $d$ divisors pair up into $\tfrac{d}{2}$ pairs that each multiply to $n$, so the product is $n^{d/2}$.

## Adding up the divisors

Expand $(1 + 2 + 4 + 8)(1 + 3 + 9)$. Each term of the result is one choice from each bracket multiplied together, which is exactly one divisor of $72$. So that product is the sum of all divisors of 72.

<Formula title="Sum of divisors">
For $n = p^a q^b \cdots$:

$$
\sigma(n) = (1 + p + \cdots + p^a)(1 + q + \cdots + q^b)\cdots
$$

</Formula>

For $72$: $(1 + 2 + 4 + 8)(1 + 3 + 9) = 15 \cdot 13 = 195$.

<Check choices={["$16$", "$24$", "$27$", "$28$", "$30$"]} answer={3} explain="$12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$, so the sum is $(1 + 2 + 4)(1 + 3) = 7 \cdot 4 = 28$. Check by listing: $1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 12 = 28$.">
What is the sum of all positive divisors of 12?
</Check>

<KeyIdea>
Whenever a problem mentions divisors, factors, multiples or "how many integers", write the prime factorization first. Almost every counting question about divisors becomes a question about **exponents**.
</KeyIdea>

<Recap>
- Every integer above 1 has one prime factorization. Find it first.
- Test primality by dividing by primes up to $\sqrt{n}$.
- Divisor count: $(a + 1)(b + 1)\cdots$. Divisor sum: multiply the sums $1 + p + \cdots + p^a$.
- Only perfect squares have an odd number of divisors.
- Product of the divisors of $n$ is $n^{d/2}$.
</Recap>
