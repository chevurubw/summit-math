title: GCD and LCM
---

The **greatest common divisor** $\gcd(a, b)$ is the largest number that divides both $a$ and $b$. The **least common multiple** $\text{lcm}(a, b)$ is the smallest positive number that both divide into. GCD answers "how big can the equal pieces be?" LCM answers "when do things line up again?"

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- find GCD and LCM from prime factorizations,
- find GCD of big numbers with the Euclidean algorithm,
- use $\gcd(a, b) \cdot \text{lcm}(a, b) = ab$,
- recognize GCD and LCM in word problems.

## From prime factorizations

Line up the primes. GCD takes the **smaller** exponent of each prime; LCM takes the **larger**.

$$
\begin{aligned}
72 &= 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \\
60 &= 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \\
\gcd &= 2^2 \cdot 3 = 12 \\
\text{lcm} &= 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 = 360
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that $12 \cdot 360 = 4320 = 72 \cdot 60$. That's always true:

<Formula>

$$
\gcd(a, b) \cdot \text{lcm}(a, b) = a \cdot b
$$

Each prime's smaller exponent plus its larger exponent equals the two exponents added together.
</Formula>

<Steps problem="Two lights blink every 12 and 18 seconds. They blink together at noon. How many times do they blink together in the next 10 minutes (not counting noon)?">
<Step>
$\text{lcm}(12, 18) = 36$ seconds between joint blinks.
</Step>
<Step>
Ten minutes is 600 seconds, and $\lfloor 600 / 36 \rfloor = 16$ (since $36 \cdot 16 = 576$).
</Step>
</Steps>

## Biggest equal pieces: GCD

Cutting a $48 \times 30$ rectangle into the largest possible equal squares with no waste? The square's side must divide both 48 and 30, and be as big as possible: $\gcd(48, 30) = 6$. You get $8 \times 5 = 40$ squares.

## The Euclidean algorithm

For big numbers, factoring is slow. Use this fact instead: **any common divisor of $a$ and $b$ also divides $a - b$**, and therefore the remainder when $a$ is divided by $b$. So $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$.

<Steps problem="Find $\gcd(1071, 462)$.">
<Step>
$1071 = 2 \cdot 462 + 147$, so $\gcd(1071, 462) = \gcd(462, 147)$.
</Step>
<Step>
$462 = 3 \cdot 147 + 21$, so it's $\gcd(147, 21)$.
</Step>
<Step>
$147 = 7 \cdot 21 + 0$. The last nonzero remainder is $21$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The trick $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(a - kb, b)$ also works with algebra. For example $\gcd(n + 7, n + 2) = \gcd(5, n + 2)$, which can only be 1 or 5.
</KeyIdea>

<Trap>
Two numbers are **relatively prime** when their GCD is 1, even if neither is prime: $\gcd(8, 15) = 1$.
</Trap>

<Recap>
- GCD: smaller exponent of each prime. LCM: larger exponent.
- $\gcd \cdot \text{lcm} = ab$.
- "Line up again" means LCM. "Largest equal pieces" means GCD.
- Euclidean algorithm: $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$; also works on expressions in $n$.
</Recap>
