---
title: Finding and counting roots
---

A polynomial of degree $n$ has exactly $n$ roots, if you count complex ones and count repeats. This lesson is about tracking them down: guessing rational roots, pairing up conjugates, and reading the graph to count the real ones.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- list every possible rational root with the Rational Root Theorem,
- use conjugate pairs to find hidden roots,
- understand repeated roots from the graph,
- count real roots by checking signs.

## The Rational Root Theorem

If a polynomial with integer coefficients has a rational root $\tfrac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms, then

$$
p \text{ divides the constant term} \quad\text{and}\quad q \text{ divides the leading coefficient.}
$$

That gives a short list of candidates to test with the Factor Theorem.

<Steps problem="Find the rational roots of $2x^3 - 3x^2 - 3x + 2$.">
<Step title="List candidates">
$p$ divides 2 and $q$ divides 2: $\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm \tfrac{1}{2}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Test">
$P(2) = 16 - 12 - 6 + 2 = 0$ ✓. $P(-1) = -2 - 3 + 3 + 2 = 0$ ✓. $P(\tfrac12) = \tfrac14 - \tfrac34 - \tfrac32 + 2 = 0$ ✓.
</Step>
<Step title="Done">
Three roots for a cubic: $2, -1, \tfrac12$. So $2x^3 - 3x^2 - 3x + 2 = (x - 2)(x + 1)(2x - 1)$.
</Step>
</Steps>

Which of these can **not** be a root of $2x^3 + 5x^2 - x + 3$, according to the Rational Root Theorem?
</Check>

## Conjugate pairs

If a polynomial has **real** coefficients and $a + bi$ is a root, then so is $a - bi$. If it has **rational** coefficients and $a + \sqrt{b}$ is a root (with $\sqrt b$ irrational), then so is $a - \sqrt{b}$.

Each pair multiplies to a nice quadratic factor:

$$
\big(x - (a + bi)\big)\big(x - (a - bi)\big) = x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 + b^2).
$$

<Check choices={["$x^2 + 4x + 5$", "$x^2 - 4x + 3$", "$x^2 - 4x + 5$", "$x^2 + 4x - 5$", "$x^2 - 2x + 5$"]} answer={2} explain="The conjugate $2 - i$ is also a root. Sum $4$, product $(2 + i)(2 - i) = 5$, so the factor is $x^2 - 4x + 5$.">
A polynomial with real coefficients has $2 + i$ as a root. Which must be a factor?
</Check>

<Check choices={["$-3$", "$-1$", "$1$", "$3$", "$6$"]} answer={3} explain="$1 - \sqrt{2}$ is also a root. The pair gives $x^2 - 2x - 1$ (sum 2, product $1 - 2 = -1$). So $P(x) = (x^2 - 2x - 1)(x - 3)$ and $P(0) = (-1)(-3) = 3$.">
$P(x)$ is a monic cubic with rational coefficients, and two of its roots are $1 + \sqrt{2}$ and $3$. What is $P(0)$?
</Check>

## Repeated roots: bounce or cross

A factor $(x - a)^m$ makes $a$ a root of **multiplicity** $m$. On the graph, an odd multiplicity **crosses** the axis and an even one **bounces** off it. Higher multiplicity flattens the curve near the root.

## Counting real roots

Between two inputs where a polynomial has opposite signs, it must cross zero at least once. Check a few values and count sign changes.

<Steps problem="How many real roots does $x^3 - 3x + 1$ have?">
<Step title="Evaluate">
$P(-2) = -1$, $P(-1) = 3$, $P(1) = -1$, $P(2) = 3$.
</Step>
<Step title="Count sign changes">
Negative to positive to negative to positive: three changes, so at least three real roots.
</Step>
<Step title="Conclude">
A cubic has at most three roots, so exactly three.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
Graphs are a counting tool. To count solutions of an equation like $x^3 = 3x - 1$, sketch both sides and count the intersections.
</Tip>

<Trap>
Complex roots of real polynomials come in pairs, so a real polynomial of **odd** degree always has at least one real root, and the number of non-real roots is always even.
</Trap>

<Recap>
- Rational roots $\tfrac{p}{q}$: $p$ divides the constant, $q$ divides the leading coefficient.
- Real coefficients: complex roots come in conjugate pairs. Rational coefficients: $a \pm \sqrt b$ pairs.
- Odd multiplicity crosses, even bounces.
- Sign changes guarantee real roots in between.
</Recap>
