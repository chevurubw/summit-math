---
title: Expected value
---

The **expected value** of a random quantity is its long run average: what you'd get per try if you repeated the experiment many, many times. It's not necessarily a value that can actually happen. The average roll of a die is 3.5, even though no face shows 3.5.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- compute an expected value from a list of outcomes,
- decide whether a game is fair,
- use **linearity of expectation**, the AMC's favorite shortcut,
- find the expected waiting time until something happens.

## The definition

Multiply each value by its probability, and add:

$$
E[X] = \sum (\text{value}) \times (\text{probability of that value}).
$$

For one fair die: $E = \tfrac{1}{6}(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) = \tfrac{21}{6} = 3.5$.

The expected value is the **balance point** of the distribution. Below is the distribution of the sum of two dice, each with $n$ sides. Change $n$: the shape changes but it always balances at $n + 1$.

A game is **fair** when the expected winnings are exactly 0.

## Linearity of expectation

<KeyIdea title="The most powerful idea in this lesson">
The expected value of a **sum** is the sum of the expected values:

$$
E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y].
$$

This is true **even when $X$ and $Y$ depend on each other**. You never need the full distribution of the sum.
</KeyIdea>

<Steps problem="A fair die is rolled 12 times. What is the expected number of sixes?">
<Step title="Split into small pieces">
Let $X_i = 1$ if roll $i$ is a six and $0$ otherwise. The number of sixes is $X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_{12}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Each piece is easy">
$E[X_i] = \tfrac{1}{6}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Add them">
$12 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} = 2$.
</Step>
</Steps>

This "count with 0 or 1 indicators" trick solves problems that look impossible:

<Steps problem="Ten people each put their hat in a pile and take one back at random. What is the expected number of people who get their own hat?">
<Step>
Person $i$ gets their own hat with probability $\tfrac{1}{10}$.
</Step>
<Step>
By linearity, the expected number is $10 \cdot \tfrac{1}{10} = 1$, even though the events are not independent.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Check choices={["$3$", "$4.5$", "$5$", "$6$", "$7.5$"]} answer={3} explain="Each of the 12 flips is heads with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$, so by linearity the expected number of heads is $12 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 6$.">
A fair coin is flipped 12 times. What is the expected number of heads?
</Check>

## Waiting times

If something happens with probability $p$ on each independent try, the expected number of tries until it first happens is

$$
\frac{1}{p}.
$$

Rolling a die until a 6 appears takes 6 rolls on average. Flipping a coin until heads takes 2 flips.

<Steps problem="Why is the waiting time $\tfrac{1}{p}$?">
<Step>
Let $E$ be the expected number of tries. You always make one try.
</Step>
<Step>
With probability $1 - p$ it fails, and you're back where you started, needing $E$ more tries on average.
</Step>
<Step>
So $E = 1 + (1 - p)E$, which solves to $pE = 1$, or $E = \tfrac{1}{p}$.
</Step>
</Steps>

That "you're back where you started" equation is a general method: write the expected value in terms of itself and solve.

<Check choices={["$2$", "$3$", "$4$", "$5$", "$6$"]} answer={2} explain="Each draw is a heart with probability $\tfrac{1}{4}$, independently, so the expected number of draws is $\tfrac{1}{1/4} = 4$.">
Cards are drawn one at a time from a well shuffled deck, **replacing** and reshuffling each time. What is the expected number of draws until the first heart?
</Check>

<Trap>
Expected value is an **average**, not a prediction. Expecting 2 sixes in 12 rolls doesn't mean you'll usually see exactly 2.
</Trap>

<Recap>
- $E = \sum \text{value} \times \text{probability}$.
- Fair game: expected winnings 0.
- Linearity: $E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$, always. Break counts into 0 or 1 indicators.
- Waiting for an event with probability $p$: $\frac{1}{p}$ tries on average.
- Harder waits: write $E$ in terms of itself.
</Recap>
