---
title: Polar form and De Moivre
---

Multiplying complex numbers with FOIL works, but it hides something beautiful. Every complex number has a **length** and a **direction**, and multiplication turns out to be simple in those terms: **multiply the lengths, add the angles.** This is the most powerful idea on AMC 12 complex number problems.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- write a complex number in polar form $r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$,
- multiply and divide by working with lengths and angles,
- raise complex numbers to high powers with De Moivre's Theorem,
- see multiplication by $i$ as a rotation.

## Length and angle

For $z = a + bi$:

- $r = |z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ is the distance from 0,
- $\theta$ is the angle from the positive real axis (the **argument**).

Then $a = r\cos\theta$ and $b = r\sin\theta$, so

$$
z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = r\operatorname{cis}\theta.
$$

Examples: $1 + i = \sqrt{2}\operatorname{cis}45^\circ$, $\ i = 1\operatorname{cis}90^\circ$, $\ -2 = 2\operatorname{cis}180^\circ$, $\ 1 + i\sqrt{3} = 2\operatorname{cis}60^\circ$.

**Why it works:** expand $\operatorname{cis}\alpha \cdot \operatorname{cis}\beta$. The real part is $\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta = \cos(\alpha + \beta)$, and the imaginary part is $\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta = \sin(\alpha + \beta)$. These are the angle addition formulas from trig.

Division works backwards: **divide the lengths, subtract the angles.**

<KeyIdea>
Multiplying by $i = \operatorname{cis}90^\circ$ rotates a point $90^\circ$ counterclockwise. Multiplying by $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ rotates by $\theta$. Rotation problems in the plane often become one multiplication.
</KeyIdea>

## De Moivre's Theorem

Multiplying $z$ by itself $n$ times multiplies the length $n$ times and adds the angle $n$ times:

$$
\big(r\operatorname{cis}\theta\big)^n = r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta).
$$

<Steps problem="Compute $(1 + i)^{8}$.">
<Step title="Polar form">
$1 + i = \sqrt{2}\operatorname{cis}45^\circ$.
</Step>
<Step title="De Moivre">
$(\sqrt{2})^8\operatorname{cis}(8 \cdot 45^\circ) = 16\operatorname{cis}360^\circ$.
</Step>
<Step title="Back to a + bi">
$\operatorname{cis}360^\circ = 1$, so the answer is $16$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Check choices={["$-64$", "$-32$", "$32$", "$64$", "$64i$"]} answer={3} explain="$1 + i\sqrt{3} = 2\operatorname{cis}60^\circ$, so its 6th power is $2^6\operatorname{cis}360^\circ = 64$.">
What is $(1 + i\sqrt{3})^6$?
</Check>

<Check choices={["$3$", "$4$", "$6$", "$8$", "$12$"]} answer={0} explain="$(1 + i)^n$ has angle $45n^\circ$. The first time that equals $135^\circ$ is $n = 3$. Check: $(1 + i)^3 = (2i)(1 + i) = -2 + 2i$, which is on the line $y = -x$ in the 2nd quadrant.">
What is the smallest positive integer $n$ for which $(1 + i)^n$ points into the 2nd quadrant along the line $y = -x$ (angle $135^\circ$)?
</Check>

<Tip>
Before grinding through $(a + bi)^n$, check whether $a + bi$ has a nice angle: $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and their relatives come from numbers like $\sqrt{3} + i$, $1 + i$, $1 + i\sqrt{3}$.
</Tip>

<Trap>
Angles are only defined up to full turns: $\operatorname{cis}390^\circ = \operatorname{cis}30^\circ$. Reduce mod $360^\circ$ at the end, and remember $\operatorname{cis}180^\circ = -1$.
</Trap>

<Recap>
- $z = r\operatorname{cis}\theta$ with $r = |z|$ and $\theta$ the angle.
- Multiply: lengths multiply, angles add. Divide: lengths divide, angles subtract.
- Multiplying by $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ is rotation by $\theta$.
- De Moivre: $(r\operatorname{cis}\theta)^n = r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta)$.
</Recap>
